A Reader's History of the Summer Game

The Long Game.

Four hundred years of cricket, told in eight chapters, one interlude, one chasm and a final over.

Cricket is more than four hundred years old, has been played on every continent except Antarctica, and can take five days to decide nothing at all. It has survived puritans, wars, bodyline bowling and the television remote — and emerged from each, somehow, more itself.

This almanac tells its story from the shepherd's field to the floodlit age. Read it straight through, or follow the red ball down the rail at the edge of the page and drop in wherever you like. When dusk falls, turn on the floodlights above.

I
Chapter One · c. 1550 – 1700

In the beginning, a shepherd's game

Nobody knows who first bowled a ball at a stick, and cricket has never minded. The likeliest cradle is the Weald — the sheep country of Kent, Surrey and Sussex — where shepherds are said to have bowled at a wicket-gate to pass the time, which is why the sticks are still called a wicket, from the old French guichet: a little gate.

The game's first confirmed appearance in writing is 1598, when a Guildford coroner named John Derrick, giving evidence in a dispute over a school plot, recalled that he and his schoolfriends had played "creckett" there "about fifty years since". By the 1640s the game was organised enough to be worth betting on: the earliest known organised match, played for a wager, is recorded in 1646. Cricket grew up in tavern company, on village greens — eleven a side, two stumps, and the stakes in a hat.

Marginalia · The first mention

Derrick's testimony of 1598 settles the game's oldest question: cricket was being played at the Royal Grammar School, Guildford, by the 1550s. Nothing older has survived in writing.

II
Chapter Two · 1700 – 1800

Writing the Laws

In 1744 the Laws of Cricket were set down for the first time: the pitch fixed at twenty-two yards, the ball weighing between five and six ounces, the umpire's word made final. They were revised in 1774 at the Star and Garter Tavern in Pall Mall — a pub, fittingly, remains the birthplace of the rulebook.

Through the 1760s and '70s the game's centre of gravity was the Hambledon Club, on Broadhalfpenny Down in Hampshire, whose eleven could beat "All-England" and drew crowds in their thousands. Then, in 1787, a group of noblemen and gentlemen moved the game to Thomas Lord's new ground in Marylebone and founded the Marylebone Cricket Club. The MCC has guarded the Laws ever since — longer than most modern nations have existed.

Marginalia · A third stump

In 1775 the bowler Edward "Lumpy" Stevens repeatedly beat the great John Small — only for the ball to fly clean through the two-stump wicket without touching it. A middle stump was added soon after. The bails, like the argument, have flown ever since.

III
Chapter Three · 1800 – 1900

Crossing oceans

Cricket travelled with ships. In 1844, the United States and Canada met at New York in what is often called the first international in any sport — Canada won by 23 runs, and few remember. English elevens toured North America in 1859 and Australia in 1861; in 1868 an Aboriginal team from western Victoria toured England, playing forty-seven matches in six months to astonished crowds.

Then the game's two defining inventions. In March 1877, at the Melbourne Cricket Ground, Australia and England met in what was later recognised as the first Test match. Australia won by 45 runs; Charles Bannerman scored 165 of Australia's 245 — still the record share of a completed Test innings. Five years later came the second invention. Australia beat England at The Oval by seven runs, and a newspaper held a funeral.

Marginalia · The urn

When Ivo Bligh's side "reclaimed the ashes" in 1882–83, a small terracotta urn was presented — said to hold the ashes of a bail, a ball, or a lady's veil, depending on the telling. It has never been an official trophy. Teams win it, then leave it in the museum at Lord's, where it belongs to cricket rather than to either country.

IV
Chapter Four · 1890 – 1914

The Golden Age

Historians still argue about many things in cricket, but rarely about this: the summers before the Great War were the game's Golden Age. In 1896 at Old Trafford, K.S. Ranjitsinhji — Ranji, an Indian prince schooled at Cambridge — made 62 and 154 not out on Test debut and gave the game its most exquisite stroke, the leg glance.

In 1902, Victor Trumper walked out at Old Trafford on a rain-ruined pitch and reached a hundred before lunch on the first morning — the first man ever to do it in a Test. Australia won that match by three runs. And in Sydney Barnes, England possessed a bowler so good he took 189 wickets in 27 Tests at 16.43, then went back to league cricket because it paid better. In 1909, England, Australia and South Africa founded the Imperial Cricket Conference at Lord's — which, under later names, runs the world game to this day.

Marginalia · Before lunch

Trumper's hundred before lunch at Old Trafford, 1902, remained the stuff of legend for a century. He batted, contemporaries wrote, "like a runner in an Olympic final" — a silk hat, it was said, was never safe near him, for he celebrated with it aloft.

V
Chapter Five · 1928 – 1948

The Don & the Bodyline storm

Donald Bradman's first Ashes series, in 1928–29, began with 18 and 1 and a dropped from the side. It was the last kindness England would be shown. In 1930 he answered with 974 runs in a single series — a record that still stands — including 334 at Leeds, 309 of them in one day.

England's reply was Bodyline: the 1932–33 tour under Douglas Jardine, with Harold Larwood bowling fast, short and at the bodies of Australian batsmen. It took the series, cost Larwood his career, cabled telegrams between prime ministers, and forced the Laws to be rewritten. War stopped play from 1939 to 1945. When cricket resumed, Bradman returned to lead the Invincibles of 1948 through England unbeaten. At The Oval, in his last innings, he walked in needing four runs to retire with a Test average of exactly 100. He was bowled by Eric Hollies for a duck — second ball. The crowd stood anyway. 99.94 it remained, and remains, the most stubborn number in sport.

Marginalia · The figure, explained

6,996 runs across 80 innings, ten times not out: 99.94. Had he made a hundred that afternoon at The Oval, the average would have risen to 101.37. He called it a "paradox" — a duck that made him more human, and the number more immortal.

VI
Chapter Six · 1950 – 1979

One-day thunder

The post-war decades belonged to long afternoons. In 1958, Gary Sobers — twenty-one years old — hit 365 not out at Kingston and the record for the highest Test innings stood for thirty-six years. But the times were about to hurry.

On 5 January 1971, after rain had eaten the first three days of a Melbourne Test, the authorities improvised a forty-over match instead. Admission was free. Australia won, nobody quite knew what had been invented, and the players wore whites and used a red ball all the same. Four years later came the first Cricket World Cup — the West Indies, Clive Lloyd's 102, three Australian run-outs, and a new world champion at Lord's.

Then, in 1977, an Australian television magnate named Kerry Packer signed the world's best players in secret and founded World Series Cricket. Courts were fought, players banned, reputations burned — and by the time peace was signed in 1979, the game had floodlights, coloured clothing, white balls and a camera at the end of a lens. The modern spectacle, for better and worse, had arrived.

Marginalia · An accident of weather

The first one-day international was an accident — a rain substitute improvised in Melbourne, with eight-ball overs and free entry. John Edrich top-scored with 82. Nobody in the ground that day knew they were attending the birth of a format that would one day decide world championships.

VII
Chapter Seven · 1980 – 2000

The world takes over

On 25 June 1983, Kapil Dev lifted the World Cup at Lord's. His India side had been 17 for 5 against Zimbabwe a fortnight earlier — Kapil answered with an unbeaten 175 — and in the final they felled the invincible West Indies by 43 runs. In India, a sport was ratified as a religion.

The map kept widening: Sri Lanka earned Test status in 1982; South Africa returned, after apartheid's exile, in 1991. In 1993, Shane Warne's first Ashes delivery drifted, ripped back and clipped Mike Gatting's off bail — cricket had a new folklore. Brian Lara made 375 at Antigua in 1994, then 400 not out a decade later. And Sachin Tendulkar, who debuted at sixteen in 1989, would retire in 2013 with 15,921 Test runs from 200 matches — numbers from a different order of devotion.

Marginalia · Ball of the century

Old Trafford, 4 June 1993. Warne's first delivery in Ashes cricket pitched a foot outside leg stump, drifted through the air, ripped off the pitch, and clipped the off bail. Gatting stared at the pitch. The umpire raised his finger. Commentary called it the Ball of the Century; nobody has bettered the name.

An Interlude · The Other Half

The women's game

Cricket history is usually told with half the players missing. It shouldn't be. The women's game is older than most of its records, and it invented the World Cup.

The earliest known women's match was reported in 1745 — "eleven maids of Bramley" against "eleven maids of Hambledon", a local paper noted, dressed in white and playing for a barrel of ale? for certain, for honour. The Women's Cricket Association formed in 1926; in 1934, England and Australia contested the first women's Test match, and the Women's Ashes began their own quiet rivalry.

And in 1973 — two years before the men — came the first Cricket World Cup of any kind: a women's tournament in England, won by the home side, driven through largely by one person. Captain, prime mover, and later a member of the House of Lords: Rachel Heyhoe Flint, cricketer, and cricket's great persuader.

Marginalia · Two sports, one athlete

Australia's Ellyse Perry is the only athlete to have represented her country at both a cricket and a football World Cup — the cricket World Cup in 2009, the football World Cup in 2011 — and then became, arguably, the finest all-rounder either sport has seen.

VIII
Chapter Eight · 2003 – Today

The T20 century

In 2003, English county cricket — facing dwindling crowds — invented Twenty20: twenty overs a side, three hours, fireworks. The first international followed in 2005, when a few New Zealanders took the field in retro kits and false moustaches, sending the format up even as they made history. By 2008, the Indian Premier League had turned cricketers into global assets and reshaped every calendar on earth.

Meanwhile the oldest format refused to die. The World Test Championship gave five-day cricket a crown; pink balls brought it into the night; and England's brazen declarations of the 2020s — "Bazball", if you must — proved the longest game could still surprise. Its proof of life came in 2019, when a World Cup final ended level after a hundred overs, level again after the Super Over, and was finally settled by counting boundaries. Cricket's most dramatic day, and its most bureaucratic ending, in one afternoon at Lord's.

Marginalia · Settled by arithmetic

14 July 2019: England 241 all out; New Zealand 241 all out; Super Over 15 each. England won on boundaries scored — 26 to 17. For the first time, a World Cup was decided by a count. Even the scorer, that day, was a kingmaker.
